Hong Kong built a place where robots have to prove themselves

Hong Kong · Physical AI · Robotics · Policy

Most robot demos are built to impress. Hong Kong has just opened a place built to find out what happens when the robot isn't having its best day.

On 15 October, FLAIR, the robotics and industrial AI centre at the Hong Kong Productivity Council (HKPC), opened the Embodied AI International Applications Hub. Embodied AI means AI built into a machine with a body, such as a robot. HKPC calls it the city's first platform for training, testing and deploying these machines, and South China Morning Post places it at the HKPC Building in Kowloon Tong.

The method is simple. Pick a task, train the robot, test it, score it, then test again. Tasks span manufacturing, logistics, retail, inspection and maintenance, and public services. HKPC plans to turn the results into standards for each industry, using measures like how often a robot finishes the job and how safely.

Seven technology partners are in the first phase, including Unitree Robotics, Robotera and Galbot. Eight international scenario partners join them, among them government departments and international companies. The release doesn't name those eight, and they're the ones we'd most like to know about.

Robotera shows what could be put to the test. It says its parcel-sorting robot handles more than 1,000 parcels an hour, with over 100 units in use, including at China Post and SF Express. That's a company claim, as reported by China Daily.

At the opening, Sun Dong, Secretary for Innovation, Technology and Industry, said the government will add HK$1 billion to its AI Subsidy Scheme, and expects to launch a HK$10 billion government-backed industry fund within the year, with AI and robotics among its themes. His speech is only in Chinese, so that's our translation.

A staged demo shows a robot on its best day. A shared test ground can give every vendor the same task and show where robots fail. So the question is whether the results get shared, and whether firms beyond the first 15 partners can use the hub.

If you ran a warehouse or a shop, which task would you hand to a robot first, and what proof would you need before you did?

Source: HKPC release via Media OutReach, 15 Oct 2026

An AI video company wants you to edit a scene by typing

Hong Kong · Generative AI · Creative industries

Imagine changing a character, a setting or a camera shot by typing a sentence, and watching the video change as you do it. That's what guests did at Cyberport on 13 October, at the opening of AIsphere's Hong Kong office. AIsphere is the company behind the PixVerse AI video platform.

The demo was PixVerse R2, which the company calls a real-time world model. In plain terms, it makes pictures and sound live as the user types. It's a company demo, so treat it as a best case.

AIsphere says it is one of the first AI video model companies to open an office in Hong Kong. It plans to hire locally and host industry events. Guests at the ribbon-cutting included Arnold Lau of Invest Hong Kong, Cyberport CEO Dr Rocky Cheng, Dickson Yeung of the Office for Attracting Strategic Enterprises, and a senior HKPC representative.

Two partnerships came with the opening. XGRIDS, a spatial intelligence company, will combine 3D reconstruction with AI video for film, tourism and real estate. The Hong Kong Polytechnic University will bring AI video into humanities teaching and responsible AI practice, with internships and joint research to follow.

The money behind it is large. Caixin reported in July that AIsphere's Series C reached RMB 2.98 billion, about US$439 million for the whole round, with Alibaba leading the latest part. PixVerse's own 14 July announcement gives the same US$439 million.

An office is cheap. What we want to see is whether the first projects with local film, tourism and retail firms ship. For a small studio, the useful question is whether live video makes it cheaper to test an idea before paying for it.

If you could turn a script into live video in minutes, what would you test first?

Source: PR Newswire, 13 Oct 2026

Bain Capital agrees up to US$250M for Hong Kong lender FundPark

Hong Kong · Fintech · Digital commerce

FundPark's pitch is that a seller's own sales data can say how much to lend them, not just a credit check. On 20 October, FundPark said Bain Capital has agreed to provide up to US$250 million in financing to the Hong Kong lender, which serves small and medium online sellers.

The money comes in two parts. US$100 million is an initial commitment, and up to US$150 million more depends on conditions. TechNode Global reports the same split.

What we don't know matters here. The release calls it financing. It doesn't say whether that's debt or equity, and it doesn't publish the terms or the conditions for the extra US$150 million.

FundPark was founded in Hong Kong in 2016. It describes its model as "Scale-Up as a Service", using AI-enabled funding and predictive data. It says it looks at real-time signals from a seller's operations to decide how much to offer. That's the company's account, as reported by TechNode Global.

Its own figures are big. FundPark says it has lent more than US$10 billion in total, that sellers on its platform have generated over US$18 billion in sales value (gross merchandise value, or GMV), and that it has supported more than 40,000 online shops.

This is money for a lender, and the AI is the company's description of how it chooses who gets how much. Loan performance, and whether the extra US$150 million is released, will show how well it works.

If your sales data could set your credit limit in real time, would you share it with a lender?

Source: Bain Capital, 20 Oct 2026
